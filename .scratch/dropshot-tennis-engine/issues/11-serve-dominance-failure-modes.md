# 11 — Failure modes, and how differently do men's and women's tennis behave?

Type: research
Status: resolved

## Question

Item 6 of ticket 05 was **not established** — the sub-agent tasked with it never returned, and nothing in that section of ticket 05 is sourced. This ticket picks it up, because the model spec cannot be finalised without it.

The single most important question: **how much does serve dominance differ between the ATP and WTA tours, and what does that imply for a shared point-level model?**

Men's tennis is materially more serve-dominated than women's. That is not a cosmetic difference — it changes the *sensitivity* of match win probability to a small change in point-win probability, because the point→game→set→match recursion is highly non-linear. A model tuned on ATP data may be badly mis-calibrated on WTA matches even if the parameterisation looks tour-neutral. Quantify this properly:
- Typical service-points-won percentages by tour, surface and era
- The resulting hold-of-serve rates and how far apart the tours actually are
- What that does to the derivative of match win probability with respect to point win probability, around each tour's typical operating point

Then the remaining failure modes:
1. **Retirements and walkovers.** How the literature handles them — discard, treat as a partial observation, or model explicitly. This matters more than it sounds: a retirement is informative about a player's state, and discarding is not neutral.
2. **Best-of-5 vs best-of-3 skill transfer.** Do players' effective skills differ across formats (fitness, tactical patience), or is the format purely a scoring difference the recursion already handles?
3. **The i.i.d. points assumption.** The Markov model assumes point outcomes are independent given the server. Klaassen & Magnus tested this directly — find what they concluded about within-match dependence and big-point effects, and how much it actually costs in predictive accuracy.
4. **Surface, altitude and ball type.** How much do altitude venues and ball changes move serve dominance, and is surface alone an adequate summary?
5. **Era effects.** How serve dominance has drifted since 1968, since the GOAT ranking depends on comparing across eras.

**Deliverable:** quantified answers with primary-source citations, and an explicit recommendation on whether ATP and WTA are one model with tour as a covariate or two separate fits. Label anything not established rather than filling gaps from recollection.

### 0. The serve-dominance gap, quantified (ESTABLISHED)

**Primary source with exact numbers:** Xiao, L., Osman, N.B. & Wang, H. (2026), "Gender Differences in Serve Performance of Professional Tennis Players in the Four Grand Slam Tournaments," *International Journal of Academic Research in Progressive Education and Development* 15(1):1546-1564. DOI: 10.6007/IJARPED/v15-i1/27660. Open access: https://hrmars.com/papers_submitted/27660/. Read directly (full PDF, all pages).

Design: final-stage matches (semifinals/finals) at all four majors, 2019-2023, 123 players (57 male, 66 female), Hawk-Eye-tracked ball/player data plus human-coded shot annotation (Cohen's kappa > 0.90, ICC 0.95-1). **Scope caveat: this is elite players at Slam finals stage, not a full-tour average** — likely biased slightly high vs. the whole tour, since better servers reach finals.

**Table 1, "serve-won" row (percentage of service points won), mean ± SD, one-way ANOVA male vs. female:**

| Major | Male | Female | F | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australian Open | 65.2% ± 10.3 | 59.0% ± 7.6 | 16.37 | <0.001 |
| Roland Garros | 61.6% ± 7.5 | 56.5% ± 8.8 | 12.86 | <0.001 |
| Wimbledon | 65.2% ± 8.1 | 59.7% ± 8.1 | 14.27 | <0.001 |
| US Open | 63.8% ± 6.7 | 58.1% ± 10.3 | 14.21 | <0.001 |

Gap is **5.5-6.2 percentage points, extremely significant at every major regardless of surface** ("court surface type did not weaken this gender difference" — paper's own conclusion). Average across the four majors: men ≈ **64.0%**, women ≈ **58.3%**.

First-serve-specific gap is even larger (1stwon row): AO 72.4% vs 68.4%; RG 67.4% vs 61.1%; WD 73.4% vs 72.9% (narrow at Wimbledon); US 72.9% vs 65.6%. Second-serve-won gap, by contrast, is **only significant at the Australian Open** (51.5% vs 44.9%, p<0.001) — RG/WD/US show no significant second-serve gender gap. So the tour-level gap is driven mostly by first-serve outcomes, not second-serve rally play — a detail that matters if a spec wants to split first/second-serve skill parameters by tour rather than a single blended serve-win rate.

**Cross-checks against independent sources (order-of-magnitude consistent, ATP side stronger-sourced than WTA):**
- Sackmann/Ingram-style ATP tour-wide average (all levels, not just Slam finalists): **≈ 62%** (Martin Ingram's blog, https://martiningram.github.io/historical-serve-return/, and Ingram 2019's fitted `theta_0` posterior median of 0.503 in a centered logit → **62.3%** for two average, equally-matched players — already cited in ticket 05 item 2). This sits slightly below the Xiao et al. 64.0% because it's a whole-tour average, not finalists only — consistent direction.
- WTA whole-tour average: only a secondary/aggregator figure was found (~57%, tennisprofits.com, unstated methodology) — **NOT independently cross-checked against a second primary source**. Treat the WTA whole-tour baseline as weaker-sourced than the ATP one; the Xiao et al. 58.3% (finalists-only) is the strongest number available for WTA and should be treated as a slight overestimate of the true whole-tour figure by the same finalist-selection logic as ATP.

**Resulting hold rates**, applying the exact `P_game(p)` closed form from ticket 05 §1a to these two operating points:

| Tour | p (service points won) | Hold rate P_game(p) |
|---|---|---|
| ATP | 0.6395 | **81.2%** |
| WTA | 0.5833 | **70.0%** |

An 11-point hold-rate gap from a 5.6-point point-win-rate gap — this is the compounding the ticket asks about, already visible one level up from the raw point stat.

#### The derivative: dP(match)/dp at each tour's operating point — computed directly

Using the exact formulas from ticket 05 (§1a game, §1b tiebreak DP, §1c set DP, §1d match), implemented and numerically verified in Python (finite-difference derivative, symmetric baseline: both players start at the tour's typical `p`, then perturb one player's serve probability by ±1e-5 and measure the response through the full game→tiebreak→set→match chain). Script and full output preserved in the session scratchpad.

| Tour | p | dP_game/dp | dP_set/dp | dP_match/dp (best-of-3) | dP_match/dp (best-of-5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ATP | 0.6395 | **1.753** | 3.321 | **4.981** | 6.226 |
| WTA | 0.5833 | **2.206** | 3.481 | **5.221** | 6.527 |

**Headline finding, stated precisely (this is more nuanced than the ticket's framing assumed, and the nuance matters for the spec):**

1. **At the game level, the derivative is 26% steeper at WTA's operating point than ATP's** (2.206 vs 1.753). This is the cleanest, most direct statement of "WTA is more sensitive to point-win-rate misestimation than ATP" — because `P_game(p)` is an S-curve, and ATP's higher `p` sits further out on the flattening tail (games are closer to deterministic holds), while WTA's `p` sits closer to the steep middle of the curve.
2. **At the match level (best-of-3, symmetric baseline), the gap compresses to only ~5%** (5.221 vs 4.981) — because match win probability is bounded in [0,1] and, for two evenly-matched players, both tours' recursions produce a match probability near 0.5 regardless of the underlying `p`, which dampens the relative derivative gap that was visible at the game level. **This is the opposite of what a naive reading of "ATP is more serve-dominated" would predict for raw match-level derivative magnitude** — the point-to-game compounding is where the real cross-tour sensitivity difference lives, not the game-to-match compounding on top of it.
3. **The "amplification factor" (dMatch/dGame, i.e. how much the point→game sensitivity gets further amplified through set/match) is larger at ATP's operating point (2.97 at p=0.65) than WTA's (2.22 at p=0.55)** in the general sensitivity sweep — i.e., ATP's higher-p regime amplifies point-level noise proportionally *more* on the way from game to match, even though its absolute game-level derivative is smaller. Both effects are real and roughly offset at the match level for evenly-matched players.
4. **Practical consequence for calibration** (computed directly, not estimated): a fixed **0.01 absolute error in estimated point-win-on-serve probability** produces roughly the same match-win-probability error at both tours' operating points in the symmetric-opponent case (ATP: 0.0498 bo3 / 0.0623 bo5; WTA: 0.0522 bo3 / 0.0653 bo5 — about a 5% relative difference, not an order of magnitude). **The place a shared model is most exposed is not "WTA match predictions blow up more than ATP ones for the same point-level error" — it is the game-level hold-rate estimate, and anywhere the model reports or is scored on hold/break statistics rather than match win probability directly.**
5. **Format effect, computed the same way:** moving from best-of-3 to best-of-5 at a *fixed* `p` increases dP_match/dp by a consistent **~25%** at both tours' operating points (ATP: 6.226/4.981 = 1.250; WTA: 6.527/5.221 = 1.250) — this is a pure mechanical consequence of needing an extra set to compound the same per-set derivative, and it is tour-invariant. This is relevant to item 2 below: even with zero true skill-transfer effect, the recursion itself makes best-of-5 predictions ~25% more sensitive to point-level misestimation than best-of-3 predictions, for identical players.

**Important scope note on what this derivative calculation is and isn't:** this uses a *symmetric* baseline (both players at the tour-typical `p`, one perturbed) to isolate the recursion's local curvature at each tour's typical point — it is not a claim about any specific match's sensitivity, and real matches involve two different `p_A`, `p_B` and a return-probability term that this simplified single-parameter sensitivity check does not vary independently. A full treatment would also perturb the returner's win-on-return probability, which is the complementary quantity Ingram's model actually estimates (`alpha - beta`, not `p` in isolation). Treat this as directionally correct and numerically exact *for the stated simplification*, not as the full joint-sensitivity surface.

### 1. Retirements and walkovers (ESTABLISHED — literature discards them, without engaging the informativeness question)

**Rates, by tour and gender, from two independent peer-reviewed 2024 studies (both read directly):**

- Oliver et al. (2024), "Retirements of professional tennis players in ATP and WTA tour events," *European Journal of Sport Science*. Open access: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsc.12177. **ATP: 0.87 retirements per 1000 games (95% CI 0.84-0.90), 3,539 retirements / 167,211 matches (2.11%). WTA: 0.81 per 1000 games (95% CI 0.70-0.88), 801 / 46,268 (1.73%).** Lowest on grass (ATP 0.67, WTA 0.52 per 1000 games), highest on hard courts (both tours 0.96). Gender pattern is **not stable over time**: women's incidence was lower than men's until ~2011, then rose sharply and "much higher for women than for men" post-2011 — the paper explicitly flags this reversal rather than reporting a single stable gender gap.
- A second paper (PLOS One 2024, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0304638), broader historical window (ATP 1978-2019, WTA 1994-2018, 584,806 + 267,380 matches): **ATP 1.56 per 1000 games (95% CI 1.54-1.59), WTA 1.36 per 1000 games (95% CI 1.33-1.39)** — a different absolute rate than the first paper (different tournament-tier scope: this one includes Challengers/ITF), but the same qualitative "men somewhat higher, gap described as inconclusive in the literature overall" pattern. Retirement risk roughly **2x higher on hard/clay than grass** for both tours.

**Modeling treatment found in the prediction literature (searched specifically, multiple papers checked directly):** universal practice is **discard**, with essentially no methodological engagement with whether that's neutral:
- Kovalchik (2016), already cited in ticket 05 item 4: excludes 105 walkovers/retirements from the 2014 ATP dataset, no further discussion.
- Wang & Drekic (2026), "Boosting markovian tennis prediction," *Journal of Sports Analytics* (via search summary — full text not directly read, treat with slightly less confidence than the directly-read sources above): explicitly removes retirement/default/walkover matches from training data.
- A 2025 arXiv Grand Slam forecasting paper (https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01613v1, read directly as HTML): states verbatim that excluded retirement matches "do not contain any relevant information for the present analysis and, hence, in order not to distort the results, are excluded" — **no engagement with the possibility that a retirement is itself informative** (about the retiring player's fitness/injury state going into the *next* match, which is exactly the ticket's concern).

**Bottom line: the "discard, treat as neutral" default is confirmed as the field-wide norm, and the ticket's underlying worry — that this isn't actually neutral — is confirmed as an unaddressed gap in the literature, not a solved problem this research missed.** No paper was found that models retirement as an informative/censored observation about player state for forecasting purposes (survival-analysis treatments exist, but for epidemiological retirement-*rate* questions, not for feeding a state-space skill estimate). **NOT ESTABLISHED: any published method for treating retirement as a partial/informative observation in a point-level or state-space model.** If the engine wants to do better than "discard," it will need to build this rather than adapt a published recipe — same situation as the scoreline-inversion gap already flagged in ticket 05 item 3.

### 2. Best-of-5 vs. best-of-3 skill transfer (NOT ESTABLISHED at the level the ticket asks)

**What was found, and why it doesn't answer the question:**
- Del Corral & Prieto-Rodríguez (2010), "Are differences in ranks good predictors for Grand Slam tennis matches?", *International Journal of Forecasting* 26(3):551-563 — studies Grand Slam (best-of-5) prediction using rank differences; found rank-difference effects are stronger among top players. This is about predictor strength by format, not about whether a player's *underlying point-win skill* shifts between formats — doesn't answer the ticket's question.
- Physiological/serve-pacing literature is genuinely mixed and doesn't isolate a skill-transfer effect: Maquirriain et al. (2016) found ATP players maintain **constant serve speed and accuracy** across five sets on grass; another study (via search summary, not directly read) found first-serve speed *was* higher in set 1 than sets 3-5, but the drop was small (0.6 m/s). A third finding (search summary): serve velocity and serve-points-won **decrease significantly in match losers but hold or increase in winners** during the 5th set — this is confounded by outcome (winners "decline less" partly *because* they're winning, not necessarily because of superior fitness), so it cannot be read as evidence of a stable skill-transfer parameter.
- Observational format-comparison claims (upset rates ~29% in best-of-3 ATP main draw vs. ~24% at Slams) are consistent with best-of-5 reducing *variance* around a fixed skill gap — which is exactly what the existing Markov recursion already predicts mechanically (more sets = more law-of-large-numbers convergence to the better player) — **not evidence that the underlying per-point skill itself changes with format.**

**What's missing:** no paper was found that isolates the two hypotheses the ticket poses — (a) format is purely a scoring-length difference the recursion already handles correctly, given the same point-win probabilities, vs. (b) players' *effective* point-win probabilities genuinely differ in best-of-5 vs. best-of-3 (fitness pacing, tactical patience, different strategic equilibrium per Paserman's point-importance argument in section 3 below, which would itself differ by scoring length since "importance" is defined via the format-specific recursion). A clean test would compare the same player's serve statistics in ATP best-of-3 (tour-level) events against that same player's best-of-5 major performance, controlling for opponent quality and round — **this analysis was not found published, and is not something this research pass can construct from search alone.**

**What is established and directly useful:** the mechanical, format-driven sensitivity difference computed in section 0 above — best-of-5 amplifies point-level estimation error into match-probability error by ~25% relative to best-of-3, at both tours' operating points, **with zero assumed skill-transfer effect**. This is a real, quantified, and tour-invariant reason the engine needs format-aware calibration even if the skill-transfer question above remains unresolved — it should be treated as a floor on the format sensitivity difference, not the whole story, since any genuine skill-transfer effect would stack on top of it.

**Practical implication for the model spec:** since women play best-of-3 everywhere including majors, and men play best-of-3 everywhere except majors, **format and tour are confounded for men only, at majors only** — an ATP player's major (bo5) serve stats are not directly comparable to their tour-level (bo3) serve stats without accounting for at least the mechanical ~25% sensitivity difference above, before even considering the unresolved skill-transfer question.

### 3. The i.i.d.-points assumption (ESTABLISHED, with a correction to a common oversimplification)

**Klaassen & Magnus's own finding (2001 JASA paper, cross-checked via multiple secondary academic summaries plus the related 2003 EJOR paper read directly in ticket 05; the primary 2001 PDF itself could not be rendered as text by the fetch tool used here — see caveat below):** points are **neither independent nor identically distributed** — winning the previous point raises the probability of winning the current point (a "momentum"/psychological effect, not just server identity), and servers do worse than their base rate specifically **at "important" points** (points whose outcome most changes match win probability — the same `importance` quantity used throughout this ticket's own derivative work). **The weaker player shows a stronger version of both effects.** Despite this, K&M's own stated practical conclusion (repeated across their 2001/2003/2014 work, and echoed by every downstream paper in ticket 05 item 3 that adopts the iid Markov chain anyway) is that the **deviation is small enough that the iid approximation remains usable for forecasting purposes** — this is the standard justification the entire point-based literature relies on to license the closed-form recursion in the first place.

**Quantified cost, from the best available primary source actually read in full (Paserman 2010/2023):** M. Daniele Paserman, "Gender Differences in Performance in Competitive Environments? Evidence from Professional Tennis Players," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 212 (2023):590-609 (originally CEPR DP 2007, this version January 2010; freely available at https://people.bu.edu/paserman/papers/Paserman_Tennis_January2010.pdf — read directly, full text). Data: stroke-by-stroke data, 7 Grand Slam tournaments, 2006-2007. Defines point "importance" identically to Klaassen & Magnus (2001) — the change in match win probability from winning vs. losing the point, computed via the same dynamic-programming Markov recursion as ticket 05 item 1.

**The headline number:** *"I estimate that, for both men and women, the probability that a player wins a match against an opponent of equal quality would increase from 0.5 to about 0.75-0.80 if he or she could avoid the deterioration in performance on more important points... This is equivalent to a more than fourfold increase in player ranking."* This is the single clearest quantified statement in the literature of what the iid violation "costs" — **eliminating the importance-driven performance drop would be worth a 25-30 percentage point match-win-probability swing for an evenly matched player.** This should be read as an upper-bound/value-of-elimination number, not a "how much predictive accuracy does an iid forecasting model lose vs. a dependence-aware one" number — **that specific comparison (delta accuracy/log-loss of an iid vs. non-iid point-level forecasting model) was searched for directly and NOT FOUND stated anywhere in the literature reviewed.** Every downstream point-based forecasting paper (Ingram 2019, Newton & Keller, Spanias & Knottenbelt) uses the iid recursion and does not report a paired comparison against a dependence-aware alternative.

**Important correction to a secondhand claim surfaced during this research:** an initial web-search summary characterized the finding as "women choke more under pressure than men." **Paserman's own primary-source conclusion, read directly, is more precise and materially different: the *mechanism* differs by gender (the ratio of unforced errors to winners rises with point importance for women but stays roughly flat for men — i.e., women's deteriorating points are more likely to end in visible unforced errors), but the *resulting probability of winning the point* deteriorates by "about the same amount for both men and women" once the full winner/error model is accounted for, and the estimated match-win-probability cost (50%→75-80%) is stated identically for both genders.** Paserman explicitly argues men's game "deteriorates at least as much" as women's, just less visibly (through more conservative shot selection rather than more errors). **This is directly relevant to the model spec: the practical magnitude of the iid violation does not appear to differ materially by tour, even though the visible symptom (error rate vs. shot selection) does** — so this is one respect in which a shared cross-tour treatment (e.g., a single "importance-adjustment" term, if the spec ever adds one) looks more justified than the raw serve-dominance numbers in section 0 would suggest on their own.

**Caveat on sourcing:** the Klaassen & Magnus (2001) JASA paper itself was not successfully rendered as readable text by the tools available in this session (SSRN, ResearchGate, and a direct janmagnus.nl PDF fetch all failed to produce parseable text); the findings attributed to it above are corroborated across multiple independent secondary academic summaries (Tilburg University research portal, Semantic Scholar, and citations within Paserman's own paper, which engages with K&M 2001's methodology directly and adopts its exact "importance" definition) rather than read verbatim from the primary PDF. Treat the qualitative conclusion (not-iid, small enough to remain usable) as solid; treat any specific numeric coefficient attributed to K&M 2001 elsewhere as unconfirmed unless it also appears in a source that was read directly.

### 4. Surface, altitude, and ball type (PARTIALLY ESTABLISHED — surface yes, altitude/ball type NOT ESTABLISHED quantitatively)

**Surface:** already well covered by ticket 05's Ingram citation (`sigma_gamma = 0.064`, surface-specific skill variance comparable in size to tournament effects) and by section 0 above, which shows the ATP/WTA serve-win gap holds "regardless of surface" per Xiao et al. (2026) — i.e., **surface does not explain away the gender gap**, but the Xiao et al. table also shows the *size* of the gap varies somewhat by surface (narrowest at Wimbledon for total serve-won, e.g. 65.2% vs 59.7%; among the widest at Roland Garros for the 1st-serve-won-specific gap). **Surface alone is not an adequate summary of serve dominance** in the sense that surface and gender interact rather than being additive — this is established directly from the ANOVA table, not inferred.

**Altitude:** only informal/anecdotal sourcing was found (tennisnerd.net and forum-level discussion of Bogotá, ~2,660m: thinner air → faster ball flight, described as "a shot lands 8.9 feet inside the baseline, carrying 9.7 mph more speed" and higher bounce, with server-friendly implications). **No peer-reviewed or primary quantitative study of altitude's effect on ace rate, service-points-won, or hold rate in professional tennis was found.** This is a real, not just under-searched, gap in the sports-science literature as far as this pass could determine — altitude tennis research exists for physiology/fatigue (VO2, recovery) but not specifically for point-outcome statistics. **NOT ESTABLISHED — needs either a bespoke analysis of ATP/WTA results at Bogotá/Quito-altitude events, or acceptance that this stays a qualitative flag (e.g. "treat Bogotá-tier altitude venues as a serve-boosting surface-like covariate, magnitude unknown") rather than a calibrated parameter.**

**Ball type:** ITF specifications confirmed (Type 1 = harder/faster, intended for slow surfaces like clay; Type 2 = standard; Type 3 = larger/slower, intended for fast surfaces) from the ITF's own equipment documentation (https://www.itftennis.com/media/2152/equipment-balls.pdf). **No study quantifying the resulting effect on serve-points-won or ace rate by ball type was found** despite a direct search. **NOT ESTABLISHED — the ITF's own stated intent (compensate for surface speed) suggests ball type is a within-surface adjustment rather than an independent lever, but no number exists to size it.**

### 5. Era drift in serve dominance since 1968 (PARTIALLY ESTABLISHED — mechanism yes, magnitude by decade no)

**Structural data constraint (already flagged in ticket 05, worth repeating precisely here because it bounds what's answerable):** per Sackmann/Ingram, ATP in-match serve statistics (serve points won, aces, etc.) are only available from **1991 onward**. Any claim about 1968-1990 serve dominance cannot be point-level; it would have to rely on indirect proxies (games-per-set variance, contemporary commentary, sparse radar-gun records) that this research pass did not locate in usable quantitative form.

**What is established, from equipment-technology research (Sheffield Hallam University research, via Choppin et al., "The Evolution of the Tennis Racket and its Effect on Serve Speed" — found via search summary and university repository record, https://shura.shu.ac.uk/2242/; not read as full text, so treat the exact figure as moderately-confident rather than fully verified):** simulated serve speed using 1970s-era rackets and balls vs. modern (2007) equipment shows an overall **~17.5% cumulative serve-speed increase since the 1870s**, with **roughly a quarter of that total change attributable to equipment changes since the 1970s specifically** — i.e., on the order of a 4-5 percentage-point serve-speed contribution from 1970s→2000s racket/string/ball technology, isolated from any change in player athleticism or technique. This is a *speed* number, not a *serve-points-won* number, and speed does not translate one-to-one into point-win probability (return technique, string technology, and coaching have also evolved on the return side).

**What is qualitatively well-supported but not numerically pinned down:** the post-2000 shift from natural gut to polyester ("poly") strings is widely credited (via search-summary-level sourcing, not a single peer-reviewed number) with making heavy topspin easier to generate, which most tennis-statistics commentary treats as a serve-and-baseline-power-favoring shift; ace rates are widely reported to have risen since the 2000s at some majors (Wimbledon specifically flagged as rising sharply around 2010, attributed to faster grass-court preparation) but **not at others** (French Open ace rates reportedly flat except where linked to independently-rising serve speed). **Explicit numeric claims of "X% ace-rate increase per decade" found during this search came only from low-trust aggregator/SEO sites with no stated methodology and were excluded from this answer as unreliable** — this is a deliberate omission, not an oversight: several such figures were found (e.g., claimed "35% rise since 2013," "20% increase over the last decade") but could not be traced to any primary or academic source, so are not reported here despite being readily searchable.

**NOT ESTABLISHED: a decade-by-decade (or even pre-/post-1991) quantified trend in service-points-won percentage, by tour.** This is a real gap. If the model spec needs an era-adjustment term for cross-era GOAT-style comparison, it will need either (a) a bespoke pull of Sackmann's match-charting/JeffSackmann GitHub data (which does go back to 1991 for the ATP and has been used for exactly this kind of trend analysis by others, per ticket 05's citation trail, but was not pulled and analyzed directly in this pass), or (b) explicit acknowledgment that pre-1991 era comparisons rest on match-outcome data only (via the scoreline-inversion approach flagged as unpublished/identifiability-limited in ticket 05 item 3), not on direct point statistics.

### Recommendation: one model with tour as a covariate, or two separate fits?

**Recommendation: neither extreme — a single hierarchical architecture (Ingram 2019-style) with tour as a top-level grouping factor carrying its own intercept and variance components, not a single pooled intercept with a tour dummy, and not two independently-tuned models that share nothing.** Reasoning, tying together the sections above:

1. **The recursion itself (ticket 05 §1) needs no duplication.** The game/tiebreak/set/match Markov formulas are pure mathematics, identical for both tours; tour-dependence enters *only* through where each tour's players cluster in `p`-space. Section 0's derivative work shows this clustering difference is real and structurally interesting (26% steeper game-level derivative for WTA, ~5% steeper match-level derivative, ~25% steeper for best-of-5 vs. best-of-3 regardless of tour) but it is a *consequence* of already-shared math applied at different operating points, not a reason to fork the recursion.
2. **A single flat covariate (one global intercept + a tour dummy) is likely insufficient**, precisely because the serve-dominance gap (section 0) is large (5.5-6.2pp, p<0.001, stable across surfaces) and sits in a region of `P_game(p)`'s curvature where the *local sensitivity itself* differs by 26% between tours. A model that pools `sigma_alpha`, `sigma_beta`, and the random-walk variances (Ingram's key hyperparameters, ticket 05 item 2) across tours risks getting both tours' calibration wrong in opposite directions if the true within-tour spread of skill differs at the different operating points — which the curvature argument in section 0 suggests is plausible even before checking real data.
3. **Two fully separate fits throw away real shared structure that the evidence here says transfers across tours:** the iid-violation ("importance") effect is, per Paserman's own primary numbers, **similar in magnitude between men and women** (50%→75-80% for both) despite differing in visible mechanism — this is a case where cross-tour pooling of a correction term looks *more* justified by the evidence than the raw serve-percentage gap would suggest. Surface effects and general aging/skill-decay curves are also plausibly transferable (nothing in this research pass found tour-specific evidence to the contrary for those components specifically).
4. **Operationally**, this means: fit `alpha` (serve skill), `beta` (return skill), and their random-walk variances **separately by tour** (i.e., tour as a grouping variable on the player-skill hierarchy, matching how Ingram already groups by time period and surface), but consider sharing or partially pooling the **surface effect (`gamma`) and importance/clutch-adjustment structure** across tours unless/until a real fit shows they diverge. This is exactly the Ingram (2019) architecture already recommended in ticket 05, extended by one more hierarchical grouping level (tour) at the layer where the evidence says it's needed (serve/return skill and its variance), and left unforked at the layer where the evidence doesn't demand it (the recursion, and plausibly the importance-adjustment).
